Product Thinking & Customer Skills

The part no technical certification covers: thinking about the business problem before the technology, and holding that ground in front of a real customer.

~90 min Prerequisite: Week 2 complete Connects to: Day 12 — Communication
Today's objective

Build a reflex: before proposing an architecture, ask yourself what business metric it moves. And practice the discovery skills an FDE uses before writing a single line of design.

Product Thinking

The most common mistake for a strong technical profile: falling in love with the solution before quantifying the problem.

Avoid"Let's implement a multi-agent system."
Prefer"The goal is to cut ticket resolution time from 12 to 3 minutes, while keeping human escalation under 15%."

The second sentence is evaluable. The first isn't. Everything you propose in an FDE interview should be reframeable in terms of: KPI, ROI, MVP, scope, and success criteria.

Customer / Consulting Skills
Discovery & Requirements Gathering

The customer doesn't always know what they need — only what hurts

Your job is to translate "we need AI" into concrete requirements, just like in the Day 09 Composition Case — the difference is there's now a real person on the other side, with their own agenda.

Handling ambiguity

Move forward with explicit assumptions, not paralysis from missing information

"I'm going to assume X because Y; correct me if I'm wrong" — is more useful than waiting for the customer to hand you a complete spec they'll probably never have.

Prioritization & Trade-offs

Not everything can be P0

An enterprise customer will always ask for more than fits in the first release. Your value is helping them decide what goes in the MVP and what waits, with explicit business criteria, not just technical ones.

Conflicts between Business, Security, and Engineering

Three legitimate agendas that rarely align from the start

Business wants speed, Security wants control, Engineering wants sustainability. Your job isn't to pick a side — it's to find the solution that satisfies each one's non-negotiable minimum.

Explaining architecture to non-technical audiences

The same diagram, two different narratives

To an engineer you talk about tools, scopes, and latency. To a business VP, the same diagram is about resolution time, risk, and cost per interaction — the architecture doesn't change, the language does.

Incidents and troubleshooting with customers

Trust is earned in the moment something goes wrong

A customer remembers how you communicated an incident more than whether the system had 99.9% or 99.5% uptime. Transparency + a concrete action plan beats a lengthy technical explanation.

Practical exercise

Written roleplay: the customer asks for "a chatbot that does everything"

An insurance company VP tells you: "We want a chatbot that answers any customer question, processes claims automatically, and is ready in 6 weeks." Write (in first person, as if speaking to them) your response covering:

  1. How you scope this down without sounding like you're saying no.
  2. What discovery questions you ask before committing to a timeline.
  3. How you tie your MVP proposal to a business metric.
See suggested approach

1. "To land something solid in 6 weeks, I propose we start with the highest-volume, lowest-risk case — claim status inquiries — and leave automatic claims processing for a second phase, once we see how it behaves in production." (Scopes down without saying "no.")

2. "What's the most frequent type of inquiry today? What percentage of claims are simple enough to process without human review? Which systems would the agent need to query — and with what level of access?"

3. "If we get 40% of status inquiries resolved without human intervention in the first 6 weeks, that frees up X hours of your support team — and gives us real data to design the automatic claims phase with much less risk."

Related interview questions
  • Tell me about a time you had to say "no" (or "not yet") to a stakeholder. How did you handle it?
  • How do you prioritize when business, security, and engineering ask for incompatible things?
  • How would you explain this same system to an engineer versus a non-technical executive?
  • Describe how you'd handle a production incident in front of an enterprise customer.
  • How do you turn "we want to use AI" into a well-defined problem?
