System Design Framework

The 16-step framework you'll use in the technical interview — memorize it as a checklist, not a rigid script.

~100 min Prerequisite: Week 1 complete Connects to: Day 07 — Distributed Systems
Today's objective

You already know how to do classic System Design. Today: the specific framework for AI cases, and practicing walking through it out loud with a guided case — the skill being evaluated isn't "you know the pieces," it's "you walk through them in order under pressure."

The 16-step framework
  1. 1. Clarify the problem Don't start designing without understanding what's actually being asked.
  2. 2. Identify users Who interacts with the system? End customers, internal agents, both?
  3. 3. Define objectives What does success mean here? Tie it to a business metric if you can.
  4. 4. Functional requirements What the system must do, in concrete terms.
  5. 5. Non-functional requirements Latency, availability, throughput, consistency.
  6. 6. Constraints Budget, existing legacy systems, compliance, deadlines.
  7. 7. High-level architecture The big boxes: channel, orchestration, integration, data.
  8. 8. Agent architecture Agent runtime, tools, memory, RAG — the Day 02 diagram.
  9. 9. Integrations Which systems it touches, with what protocol, sync or async.
  10. 10. Data What gets persisted, where, with what model (RAG, transactional, both).
  11. 11. Security Identity, scopes, guardrails — Day 04 applied to the case.
  12. 12. Resilience What happens when something fails — Day 05 applied to the case.
  13. 13. Observability How you'd know something went wrong before the customer tells you.
  14. 14. Scalability What breaks first if traffic multiplies by 10.
  15. 15. Trade-offs Name the alternatives you discarded and why.
  16. 16. Rollout How you launch this without betting everything at once — canary, feature flags, pilot.
How to use it under pressure

Don't recite it from memory in the interview — use it as a mental map so you don't freeze. The most common mistake isn't "not knowing the architecture," it's jumping straight to step 7 without clarifying the problem first. The interviewer is evaluating your process as much as your result.

Practical ruleSpend at least 3-5 minutes on steps 1-6 before drawing a single box. A candidate who clarifies well and designs something simple beats one who designs something elaborate for the wrong problem.
Guided practice case

Prompt: "Design an agent that helps sales reps prepare for meetings."

It's intentionally ambiguous. Walk through the first 6 steps out loud, as if you were in the interview, before looking at the suggested approach.

See suggested approach for steps 1-6

1. Clarify: "Does 'prepare' mean summarizing the customer's history, suggesting talking points, or both? Is it before the meeting or also during?" — never assume scope.

2. Users: Sales reps (primary user). Possibly managers who review prep quality (secondary user).

3. Objective: Cut prep time from 30 minutes to 5, while maintaining or improving meeting quality — a concrete KPI, not "use AI."

4. Functional: Summarize CRM history, recent interactions, recent news about the customer's company, suggest 3 talking points.

5. Non-functional: Must be ready <30s before the meeting starts (triggered by the calendar), high availability during business hours, doesn't need to be real-time during the meeting itself.

6. Constraints: Existing CRM (Salesforce), customer data is sensitive (can't leave the corporate network), non-technical sales team.

Related interview questions
  • Why clarify the problem before designing, even if you think you understand it?
  • How do you decide which non-functional requirements matter most for a given case?
  • What do you do if the interviewer doesn't give you enough information?
  • How do you present trade-offs without sounding indecisive?
  • Why is rollout part of the design and not a separate implementation detail?
